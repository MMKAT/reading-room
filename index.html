Frameworks: the ready reckoner

One glance before a session. Each card: the idea, when to reach for it, one question it gives you, and its limits. The two marked NEW may be unfamiliar; they earn their place.

In the conversation

GROW Whitmore

The idea. Goal, Reality, Options, Will: the default arc for a coaching conversation.
Reach for it when a conversation wanders or a coachee wants visible structure.
Ask: "What have you already tried?"
Limits. Mechanical if followed rigidly, and it assumes the goal is the right one, which is often the real coaching question.

Reflective inquiry Reynolds

The idea. Reflect back what you heard (words, emotion, contradiction) and let the summary do the provoking, instead of stacking clever questions.
Reach for it when you notice yourself working harder than the coachee.
The move is a statement, not a question: "You said X, and a minute later Y."
Limits. Needs precision; a sloppy reflection derails more than a mediocre question.

Understanding the person

Johari Window Luft & Ingham

The idea. Four panes of self-knowledge: open, blind, hidden, unknown.
Reach for it in 360 debriefs: the blind pane is what the 360 exists to shrink.
Ask: "What might others see that you can't?"
Limits. A static snapshot; says nothing about what to do next.

Maslow's hierarchy

The idea. Needs ordered from survival to self-actualization; unmet lower needs dominate attention.
Reach for it when a leader's stated ambition conflicts with their current security (role at risk, reorganization).
Ask: "What feels at stake for you right now, really?"
Limits. The strict ordering has weak empirical support; a lens, not a law.

Achievement, affiliation, power McClelland

The idea. People are driven by different dominant needs, and leaders over-index on achievement.
Reach for it when a coachee struggles to delegate or to enjoy leading.
Ask: "What part of the last win felt best: the result, the relationships, or the influence?"
Limits. Profiles drift by context; avoid typing people permanently.

Attachment theory at work Bowlby

The idea. Early relational patterns shape how adults respond to support and threat: secure, anxious, avoidant.
Reach for it when a coachee's reaction to a boss or board seems bigger than the situation.
Ask: "What do you typically do when support feels uncertain?"
Limits. Coaching, not therapy; use it to normalize a pattern, never to diagnose.

Immunity to Change Kegan & Lahey

The idea. People don't resist change out of weakness; they are loyal to a hidden competing commitment that protects them from a feared outcome.
Reach for it when a motivated coachee keeps not doing the thing.
Ask: "What would you have to risk feeling if you actually changed this?"
Limits. Takes real time; not a five-minute tool.

Leading others

Situational Leadership Hersey & Blanchard

The idea. Match style (directing, coaching, supporting, delegating) to the person's competence and commitment for this task.
Reach for it on the classic "coach or direct?" dilemma.
Ask: "What does this person need from you on this task right now: direction or trust?"
Limits. Readiness is task-specific and moves; reassess constantly.

Secure Base Leadership Kohlrieser

The idea. Leaders who combine caring and daring become the base from which people take risks.
Reach for it with leaders who are either all warmth or all challenge.
Ask: "Who is your secure base, and whose secure base are you?"
Limits. Familiar program vocabulary; push past the words to actual behaviors.

Psychological safety Edmondson

The idea. The shared belief that interpersonal risk-taking is safe; built by framing work as learning, admitting fallibility, and asking questions.
Reach for it when a coachee's team is quiet, compliant, or hiding problems.
Ask: "When did someone last bring you bad news early, and what happened to them?"
Limits. Safety is not comfort or low standards; pair it with accountability.

SCARF Rock · NEW

The idea. Five social triggers the brain treats like survival threats: status, certainty, autonomy, relatedness, fairness.
Reach for it to decode why a reasonable change produced an unreasonable reaction.
Ask: "Which of the five felt attacked?"
Limits. The neuroscience framing is looser than it sounds; the checklist is the value.

Participative decision model Vroom & Yetton

The idea. Decide alone, consult, or decide together, chosen by decision significance, information location, and needed commitment.
Reach for it with leaders who default to one mode for every decision.
Ask: "Who holds the information, and whose commitment do you need?"
Limits. Assumes time to choose a mode; crises collapse it.

Organizations and groups

Levels of culture Schein

The idea. Artifacts on the surface, espoused values beneath, underlying assumptions at the bottom, and the bottom layer wins.
Reach for it when a change effort keeps bouncing off "how things are done here."
Ask: "What does the organization actually do when no one is watching?"
Limits. Assumptions are inferred, not observed; hold hypotheses lightly.

Basic assumptions in groups Bion · NEW

The idea. Under pressure, groups slide from work mode into dependency (waiting for a savior), fight-flight (an enemy to blame), or pairing (hoping two members will fix it).
Reach for it when observing board simulations: name the mode, not the individuals.
Ask yourself: "What is this group doing instead of the task?"
Limits. Tavistock language can alienate; translate before using it with participants.

From reading to practice

A reading only counts when it changes a conversation. Two small rituals keep that honest.

After you read: the one-page note

  1. The key argument, in two sentences.
  2. What it changes in a coaching conversation.
  3. One move or question you can use.
  4. Its limits: when not to use it.
  5. At most one quotable finding, with its source.

Over time these notes become your own ready reckoner: the thing you glance at before a session.

After a session: the ten-minute review

  1. Quick estimates: coachee talk-time, questions vs statements, frameworks offered, silences held.
  2. What did you hear that was not said, and did you use it?
  3. Where did you step in when silence would have served better?
  4. Which single question created the shift, and why did it work?
  5. One behavior for next session. Exactly one.

No names, no identifying details, ever. Just the craft.
