Frameworks: the ready reckoner

One glance before a session. Tap a card to open it: full definition, the picture, when to reach for it, one question, limits, and where to go deeper. The two marked NEW may be unfamiliar; they earn their place.

In the conversation

GROW Whitmore

▼

Goal, Reality, Options, Will: the default arc for a coaching conversation.

Definition. A four-stage conversation structure: agree what the coachee wants (Goal), examine what is actually happening (Reality), generate possibilities without judging them (Options), and commit to specific action (Will). Popularized by Sir John Whitmore in Coaching for Performance; the most widely used coaching model in organizations.

Reach for it when a conversation wanders or a coachee wants visible structure.
Ask: "What have you already tried?"
Limits. Mechanical if followed rigidly, and it assumes the goal is the right one, which is often the real coaching question.

Go deeper: The GROW model (Performance Consultants)

Reflective inquiry Reynolds

▼

Reflect back what you heard and let the summary do the provoking, instead of stacking clever questions.

Definition. A coaching stance in which the coach's main instrument is the reflection: playing back the coachee's words, emotions, gestures, and contradictions so the coachee can examine their own thinking from the outside. Questions follow reflections rather than lead them. Marcia Reynolds' Coach the Person, Not the Problem is the definitive treatment.

Reach for it when you notice yourself working harder than the coachee.
The move is a statement, not a question: "You said X, and a minute later Y."
Limits. Needs precision; a sloppy reflection derails more than a mediocre question.

Go deeper: Reflective inquiry (Co-Active) The book's home (Covisioning)

Understanding the person

Johari Window Luft & Ingham

▼

Four panes of self-knowledge: open, blind, hidden, unknown.

Definition. A two-by-two map of self-awareness built from two questions: what is known to me, and what is known to others. Feedback shrinks the blind pane; disclosure shrinks the hidden pane; both grow the open pane where trust and effectiveness live.

Reach for it in 360 debriefs: the blind pane is what the 360 exists to shrink.
Ask: "What might others see that you can't?"
Limits. A static snapshot; says nothing about what to do next.

Go deeper: Johari window (overview)

Maslow's hierarchy

▼

Needs ordered from survival to self-actualization; unmet lower needs dominate attention.

Definition. Motivation as a rough ladder: physiological needs, safety, belonging, esteem, and self-actualization. The coaching value is the reminder that a threatened lower need (job security, belonging in a new team) will quietly dominate a leader's attention however lofty their stated goals.

Reach for it when a leader's stated ambition conflicts with their current security (role at risk, reorganization).
Ask: "What feels at stake for you right now, really?"
Limits. The strict ordering has weak empirical support; a lens, not a law.

Go deeper: Maslow's hierarchy (Simply Psychology)

Achievement, affiliation, power McClelland

▼

People are driven by different dominant needs, and leaders over-index on achievement.

Definition. David McClelland's learned-needs theory: adults develop a dominant motivational need for achievement (mastery, results), affiliation (belonging, harmony), or power (influence, impact), and it shapes what energizes and what drains them. Effective senior leadership often requires shifting weight from achievement toward socialized power: impact through others.

Reach for it when a coachee struggles to delegate or to enjoy leading.
Ask: "What part of the last win felt best: the result, the relationships, or the influence?"
Limits. Profiles drift by context; avoid typing people permanently.

Go deeper: McClelland's need theory (overview)

Attachment theory at work Bowlby

▼

Early relational patterns shape how adults respond to support and threat: secure, anxious, avoidant.

Definition. John Bowlby's insight that humans form internal working models of relationships, carried into adulthood: secure (support is available, so risk is possible), anxious (support is uncertain, so seek reassurance), avoidant (support is unreliable, so go it alone). At work these patterns surface under pressure, in how leaders relate to bosses, boards, and their own teams.

Reach for it when a coachee's reaction to a boss or board seems bigger than the situation.
Ask: "What do you typically do when support feels uncertain?"
Limits. Coaching, not therapy; use it to normalize a pattern, never to diagnose.

Go deeper: Attachment theory (Simply Psychology)

Immunity to Change Kegan & Lahey

▼

People don't resist change out of weakness; they are loyal to a hidden competing commitment.

Definition. A diagnostic map with four columns: the improvement goal, the behaviors that work against it, the hidden competing commitment those behaviors serve (usually protection from a feared outcome), and the big assumption that makes the fear feel true. Change happens by testing the assumption, not by trying harder.

Reach for it when a motivated coachee keeps not doing the thing.
Ask: "What would you have to risk feeling if you actually changed this?"
Limits. Takes real time; not a five-minute tool.

Go deeper: The Real Reason People Won't Change (HBR)

Leading others

Situational Leadership Hersey & Blanchard

▼

Match style (directing, coaching, supporting, delegating) to the person's readiness for this task.

Definition. Leadership style is not a personality but a choice along two dials: how much direction you give and how much support. The right setting depends on the person's competence and commitment for this specific task, so the same person may need directing on one task and delegating on another.

Reach for it on the classic "coach or direct?" dilemma.
Ask: "What does this person need from you on this task right now: direction or trust?"
Limits. Readiness is task-specific and moves; reassess constantly.

Go deeper: Situational Leadership (Center for Leadership Studies)

Secure Base Leadership Kohlrieser

▼

Leaders who combine caring and daring become the base from which people take risks.

Definition. Drawing on attachment theory and hostage negotiation, George Kohlrieser argues a leader's job is to be a secure base: enough bonding that people feel safe, enough challenge that they leave the comfort zone. Care without dare protects but doesn't stretch; dare without care drives but doesn't hold.

Reach for it with leaders who are either all warmth or all challenge.
Ask: "Who is your secure base, and whose secure base are you?"
Limits. Familiar program vocabulary; push past the words to actual behaviors.

Go deeper: Kohlrieser and secure base leadership (overview)

Psychological safety Edmondson

▼

The shared belief that interpersonal risk-taking is safe.

Definition. Amy Edmondson's finding that team learning depends on a shared belief that speaking up, admitting error, and asking questions will not be punished. Crucially it pairs with standards: high safety plus high standards produces learning; high safety alone produces comfort.

Reach for it when a coachee's team is quiet, compliant, or hiding problems.
Ask: "When did someone last bring you bad news early, and what happened to them?"
Limits. Safety is not comfort or low standards; pair it with accountability.

Go deeper: What is psychological safety? (HBR) Overview

SCARF Rock · NEW

▼

Five social triggers the brain treats like survival threats.

Definition. David Rock's model of the five social domains the brain monitors like physical threats: status, certainty, autonomy, relatedness, fairness. A threat in any domain narrows thinking and triggers defense; a reward broadens it. Useful shorthand for why a "reasonable" reorganization lands like an attack.

Reach for it to decode why a reasonable change produced an unreasonable reaction.
Ask: "Which of the five felt attacked?"
Limits. The neuroscience framing is looser than it sounds; the checklist is the value.

Go deeper: The SCARF model (NeuroLeadership Institute)

Participative decision model Vroom & Yetton

▼

Decide alone, consult, or decide together, chosen deliberately, not by habit.

Definition. A decision about the decision: how much participation this particular choice needs, based on its significance, where the information sits, and whose commitment the outcome requires. The model's value is making the mode a conscious choice instead of a personality default.

Reach for it with leaders who default to one mode for every decision.
Ask: "Who holds the information, and whose commitment do you need?"
Limits. Assumes time to choose a mode; crises collapse it.

Go deeper: Vroom-Yetton model (overview)

Organizations and groups

Levels of culture Schein

▼

Artifacts on the surface, espoused values beneath, underlying assumptions at the bottom, and the bottom layer wins.

Definition. Edgar Schein's three-level model of organizational culture: what you can see (artifacts: offices, rituals, org charts), what people say they believe (espoused values), and what they actually assume without noticing (underlying assumptions). Change efforts that address only the top layers bounce off the bottom one.

Reach for it when a change effort keeps bouncing off "how things are done here."
Ask: "What does the organization actually do when no one is watching?"
Limits. Assumptions are inferred, not observed; hold hypotheses lightly.

Go deeper: Edgar Schein's culture model (overview)

Basic assumptions in groups Bion · NEW

▼

Under pressure, groups slide from work mode into dependency, fight-flight, or pairing.

Definition. Wilfred Bion's Tavistock observation that a group is always doing two things: the task, and managing its own anxiety. When anxiety wins, the group unconsciously behaves as if its purpose were something else: waiting for a savior (dependency), finding an enemy (fight-flight), or hoping two members will produce the answer (pairing). Naming the mode releases the group back to work.

Reach for it when observing board simulations: name the mode, not the individuals.
Ask yourself: "What is this group doing instead of the task?"
Limits. Tavistock language can alienate; translate before using it with participants.

Go deeper: Bion and group dynamics (overview)

From reading to practice

A reading only counts when it changes a conversation. Two small rituals keep that honest.

After you read: the one-page note

  1. The key argument, in two sentences.
  2. What it changes in a coaching conversation.
  3. One move or question you can use.
  4. Its limits: when not to use it.
  5. At most one quotable finding, with its source.

Over time these notes become your own ready reckoner: the thing you glance at before a session.

After a session: the ten-minute review

  1. Quick estimates: coachee talk-time, questions vs statements, frameworks offered, silences held.
  2. What did you hear that was not said, and did you use it?
  3. Where did you step in when silence would have served better?
  4. Which single question created the shift, and why did it work?
  5. One behavior for next session. Exactly one.

No names, no identifying details, ever. Just the craft.
